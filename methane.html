Methane Plume Capture and Reuse Scoring

Finding a methane leak from orbit is the easy half. The harder question is whether anything near the leak could burn the gas if it were captured — because a plume with no demand within reach is purely an emissions problem, while a plume beside a load that wants energy is a supply opportunity. This project scores plumes on that second question.

The gap

Satellite methane monitoring is mature, and GIS work built on it is almost entirely retrospective: mapping hotspots, attributing them to operators, and supporting leak detection and repair. That serves regulators well. It is close to useless to anyone deciding where capture infrastructure would pay for itself. This work runs the analysis forward instead — treating plume detections as a siting problem rather than a compliance one.

Study area

A 200 km² subregion of the Appalachian-Marcellus basin, spanning parts of Pennsylvania, Ohio and West Virginia. The region carries thousands of well pads, processing stations and transmission pipelines, so both intentional venting and fugitive leaks are dense; its mix of industrial zones and rural-to-semi-urban commercial development means demand varies sharply over short distances, which is what makes proximity worth modelling at all.

Method

  • Plume detections. MethaneSAT L4 point data from the Environmental Defense Fund public preview, filtered to a single satellite overpass, keeping leaks above 500 kg/h with low retrieval uncertainty. Each carries a flux estimate and its standard deviation.
  • Demand surface. Infrastructure polygons from OpenStreetMap, classified as industrial, commercial, data centre or other, each given an energy density scaled by footprint area — data centres weighted highest, at roughly four times a generic parcel.
  • Proximity join. Each plume is buffered to ten kilometres and spatially joined to every infrastructure feature inside that radius.
  • Distance weighting. Matched demand is combined by inverse distance and weighted by land use, so a data centre two kilometres away counts for far more than a commercial parcel at the buffer’s edge. The economics of moving captured gas fall off sharply with distance, and the weighting is what encodes that.
  • Feasibility ratio. Energy recoverable from the plume divided by that weighted demand, capped at 1.0.
  • Composite score. Flux, measurement uncertainty, the feasibility ratio and avoided CO2-equivalent emissions combine into one capture score, binned into high, moderate and low potential tiers.

Why data centres are weighted highest

Not an arbitrary constant. Data centre power demand is projected to grow by roughly 160% by 2030, against the 1–2% of global electricity the sector consumes today — growth driven largely by AI workloads. A methane plume next to that kind of load is a materially different proposition from one next to a warehouse, and the weighting says so explicitly rather than burying it.

The design decision worth arguing about

The whole analysis stays inside Google Earth Engine, which constrains how much data it can process — and is why the study area is 200 km² rather than a basin or a continent. Rebuilt in ArcGIS or QGIS the same method would run globally.

That limit was accepted deliberately. The point was a method anyone could re-run for free, with modest GIS skills and no licensed software, on a public dataset. A global result nobody else can reproduce is worth less than a small one that any regional planner can run over their own patch tomorrow. Scope was the thing traded away; replicability was the thing bought.

What it does not do

  • Energy demand is inferred from OSM land use, which flattens real variation and ignores temporal swings and off-grid supply.
  • Capping the feasibility ratio at 1.0 stops low-demand areas skewing the ranking, but it also hides places where surplus gas could be stored or exported.
  • Avoided emissions assume full capture and complete combustion at a 25× global warming potential. Real capture efficiency varies.
  • Nothing here models permitting, site access, retrofit cost or jurisdiction — all of which can decide feasibility on their own.

It is a first-pass filter for ranking plumes against each other, to be followed by cost-benefit analysis and site verification. Satellite flux figures and OSM footprints both need confirming on the ground.
