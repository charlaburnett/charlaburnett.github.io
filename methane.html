Methane Plume Capture and Reuse Scoring

Finding a methane leak from orbit is the easy half. The harder question is whether anything near the leak could actually burn the gas if it were captured — because a plume with no demand within reach is an emissions problem, while a plume next to a load that wants energy is a supply opportunity. This project scores plumes on that second question.

Approach

The analysis runs over the Appalachian Basin — eastern Ohio, western Pennsylvania and northern West Virginia — and joins satellite-detected methane plumes to the energy infrastructure sitting around them.

  • Plume detections. MethaneSAT L4 point data from the Environmental Defense Fund public preview, filtered to the study area, carrying a flux estimate and its standard deviation for each plume.
  • Demand surface. Infrastructure polygons classified as industrial, commercial, data centre or other, each assigned an energy-density estimate scaled by footprint area — data centres weighted highest, at roughly four times a generic parcel.
  • Proximity join. Each plume is buffered to ten kilometres and spatially joined to every infrastructure polygon within that radius.
  • Distance weighting. Matched demand is combined by inverse distance and weighted by land use, so a data centre two kilometres away counts for far more than a commercial parcel at the edge of the buffer.
  • Feasibility ratio. The energy recoverable from the plume itself is divided by that weighted demand and capped at one, which distinguishes a leak that could meaningfully serve nearby load from one that is negligible against it.
  • Scoring. Flux, measurement uncertainty, the feasibility ratio and avoided CO2-equivalent emissions combine into a single capture score, binned into high, moderate and low potential tiers.

Why it is framed this way

Most methane remote sensing stops at detection and quantification, which serves regulators and reporting frameworks well. It is less useful to anyone deciding where capture infrastructure would pay for itself. Ranking plumes by the demand within reach of them turns a monitoring product into a siting one, and the distance weighting matters because the economics of moving captured gas fall off sharply with pipeline distance.

Status

Written as a Google Earth Engine script. The energy-density and land-use weights are first-pass estimates intended to rank plumes against each other rather than to produce absolute energy figures, and the scoring would need calibration against real capture project costs before it carried decision weight.
